28, Starting Over, and Scared

This is either the smartest decision of my life or the one that finally proves everyone right about me.

It's March 2026.

I'm 28.

My friends and cousins are abroad, building careers, buying apartments, stacking stocks. Some of them seem to have figured out the great adult game while I am still daydreaming like tomorrow isn't coming.

And I've just enrolled in a second bachelor's degree.

Not an MBA. Not some six-month certification I can throw onto LinkedIn.

An actual undergraduate degree.

Online.

With a plan that could keep me in university until I'm 38 — maybe 40.

At 28, I'm voluntarily starting from the beginning again.

And, honestly, I'm terrified.

The bug I never got rid of

I was always good at maths.

But somewhere around twelfth grade, I watched The Social Network on loop and caught a different kind of bug.

I wanted to build something.

So I did.

A couple of friends and I started a subscription meal business. It made just enough money to fund a respectable amount of beer and give us the intoxicating feeling that maybe we were onto something.

Then our investor pulled out.

The money disappeared. The delivery system collapsed with it.

Around the same time, I ended up in the wrong company.

And that chapter closed.

Then music got me.

Rap had this particular gravity to it — the braggadocio, the materialism, the ridiculous ambition wrapped in bass. I became a music producer and mixing engineer. First from my bedroom, then in Mumbai, working with whoever would let me into the room.

For a while, it worked.

Our little group of producers accumulated hundreds of thousands of streams. I spent my days around artists, songwriters, engineers and people convinced they were going to make it.

Then COVID arrived.

The studios shut down.

My mixing job disappeared.

I moved back in with my parents.

And somewhere in the middle of lockdown, while I was aimlessly killing time, a friend showed me freeCodeCamp.

I started building websites.

Then I couldn't stop.

Web development became software engineering, and eventually software engineering became my first real job.

I didn't take it seriously enough.

I didn't know what was at stake.

The years I don't regret losing

Then I drifted again.

For a few years, I helped run my father's real estate business. Software disappeared from my life almost completely.

Looking back, I spent roughly five or six years jumping between identities.

Business.

Music.

Code.

Family.

Back to code.

On paper, it looks like a spectacular waste of time.

It wasn't.

I spent afternoons in parks around Pali Hill with upcoming artists. I shared apartments with songwriters and actors. I met Manoj Bajpayee at the studio where I worked. I built a business with my best friend. I ate my way through Mumbai.

There were friendships. Mistakes. Long nights. Bad decisions. Great ones.

There was a life.

I wouldn't erase any of it.

But I can't pretend it didn't cost me something.

Because somewhere in the middle of all of that, the world changed.

ChatGPT happened.

And I was busy helping run my father's business.

I wasn't watching closely.

Then I looked up.

It was 2025.

And it felt like everyone else had already made their move.

The table was full.

I was walking into the room late.

Why not just get another software job?

I could.

I've done it before.

I could polish my résumé, grind LeetCode, apply to a few thousand companies and eventually get ghosted by most of them.

And maybe that's exactly what I should do.

But something about it feels wrong.

I don't want to spend the next decade building another AI agent wrapper for whatever industry has the biggest budget that year.

I want to understand the thing underneath.

The models.

The mathematics.

The systems.

The research.

The frontier.

If this were 2012, I'd probably be building a Web 2.0 startup.

If it were 2024 and I knew an industry inside out, I'd probably build an agent for it.

But it isn't 2012.

It isn't 2024.

The frontier moved.

And I'm trying to move with it.

The actual fear

Here's the part I'm almost embarrassed to admit.

I'm scared of AI.

Not in the science-fiction, killer-robots kind of way.

I'm scared of becoming irrelevant.

If AGI arrives before I've positioned myself to participate in whatever comes next, I could lose.

Not "I'm five years behind" lose.

Not "I need to learn a new framework" lose.

Sidelined.

Permanently.

I don't know if AGI is coming in five years, twenty years, or whether the whole premise is wrong.

I'm not an economist. I'm not a futurist.

It's a bet.

A bet based on watching what today's systems have already done to software, knowledge work and the economics of building things.

And it's a bet I take seriously enough to reorganize my life around it.

Starting over

So here I am.

Twenty-eight years old.

A second bachelor's degree.

Online.

From home.

No awkwardly sitting in a lecture hall surrounded by eighteen-year-olds.

But also no professors who know me well enough to write the kind of recommendation letter that opens the door to a top PhD program.

Every choice has a price.

This one does too.

I'm trading speed for depth.

Comfort for optionality.

The conventional path for a longer one.

Maybe I'm making the smartest decision of my life.

Maybe I'm spectacularly late.

Maybe, ten years from now, I'll look back at this and laugh at how scared I was.

Or maybe I'll look back and realize this was the moment everything changed.

I don't know.

That's the uncomfortable part about starting over.

There is no montage where the music swells and you suddenly become the person you wanted to be.

There's just you.

A little behind.

A little embarrassed.

A little scared.

Standing at the beginning of a road you have no idea how to finish.

And walking anyway.

I'm betting the next decade on getting close enough to the frontier that the fear stops applying to me.

I don't have a tidy ending for this story.

Not yet.

This is just the beginning.

And I'm scared.

I'm doing it anyway.

— Dheeraj
